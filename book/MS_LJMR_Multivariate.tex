\chapter*{Preface}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Preface}
\markboth{Preface}{}

This book has one governing idea: \emph{almost everything in multivariate analysis
is a statement about a data matrix and the geometry of its rows and columns}. A table
of $n$ objects measured on $p$ variables can be read two ways — the \emph{Q view}
(relationships among objects) and the \emph{R view} (relationships among variables) —
and the great methods of the field are, at bottom, different ways of decomposing that
one matrix. Principal components, correspondence analysis, discriminant analysis and
even modern machine-learning embeddings all fall out of the same small set of linear-
algebra moves: centering, scaling, distance, and eigen/singular-value decomposition.

The material grew out of Dr. Jorge Sober\'on's Multivariate Methods course. The revised
Fall-2026 schedule folds Principal Coordinate Analysis into the Principal Components
sequence, retires the standalone visualization sessions, and adds four sessions on
machine learning. This edition follows that schedule. It also rebuilds the course's \R{}
scripts into a single, portable package, \code{MS\_LJMR/}, that runs on Windows, macOS,
Linux and in batch mode without editing a single path.

Each chapter follows the same rhythm: the \emph{idea} and its mathematics; the \emph{code}
that expresses it; the \emph{output} it produces; and an \emph{interpretation} that says
what the numbers and pictures mean. Boxes flag the one thing worth remembering
(\textcolor{wnavy}{\textbf{Key idea}}), the trap that catches everyone
(\textcolor{wwine}{\textbf{Pitfall}}), and the places where the machine-learning course
connects back to the classical theory (\textcolor{wteal}{\textbf{From the ML course}}).

\chapter{The Multivariate Data Matrix}
% ===========================================================================
\section{Two views of one table}
A multivariate data set is a matrix $\mathbf{X}$ with $n$ rows (objects, sites,
specimens, samples) and $p$ columns (variables, traits, features). Nearly every
technique in this book answers one of two questions about $\mathbf{X}$, historically distinguished in ecological literature as the Q and R modes \cite{Legendre_2012}:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Q~mode.} How similar are the \emph{objects} (rows)? This mode focuses on comparing the sample units, calling for a \emph{dissimilarity} or distance between rows. It is the natural view when seeking to cluster or ordinate sites based on their species composition or objects based on their properties.
  \item \textbf{R~mode.} How associated are the \emph{variables} (columns)? This mode focuses on the relationships among descriptors, calling for a \emph{covariance} or correlation between columns. It is the natural view when aiming to reduce the number of variables by finding latent structures or underlying gradients.
\end{itemize}

\begin{keybox}
The non-zero eigenvalues of $\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\!\top}$ (an $n\times n$, Q-mode matrix)
and of $\mathbf{X}^{\!\top}\mathbf{X}$ (a $p\times p$, R-mode matrix) are \emph{identical}.
This single fact is why the same decomposition can be read as an ordination of objects or
of variables, and it underlies PCA, correspondence analysis and biplots alike.
\end{keybox}

\section{Centering, scaling, and why units matter}
Raw variables usually live on incompatible scales (millimetres vs.\ millimetres of
rainfall). Two operations put them on common footing. \emph{Centering} subtracts each
column mean, moving the origin to the centroid; \emph{scaling} divides by each column
standard deviation, making variances comparable. The combined $z$-transform,
\[
  z_{ij}=\frac{x_{ij}-\bar x_j}{s_j},
\]
is the default preprocessing for correlation-based methods. In \R{} it is one call,
\code{scale()}, and the covariance of the scaled matrix \emph{is} the correlation matrix
of the original.

\section{Distance and dissimilarity}
For the Q view we need a distance $d(i,h)$ between rows $i$ and $h$. The workhorse is
Euclidean distance on standardized data,
\[
  d(i,h)=\sqrt{\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{p}(z_{ij}-z_{hj})^2},
\]
but Euclidean distance often fails in ecological contexts, particularly due to the ``double-zero problem'' where joint absences do not imply similarity \cite{Legendre_2012}. Therefore, count and presence/absence data demand other metrics such as the Bray--Curtis dissimilarity \cite{Bray_1957}, Jaccard, or $\chi^2$ distance. When working with mixed numeric and categorical data, Gower's general coefficient of similarity \cite{Gower_1971} is required because it gracefully handles missing values and variables on completely different scales by standardizing each pairwise comparison by the variable's range. Ultimately, choosing the distance is the single most consequential modelling decision in ordination — it decides what ``close'' means before any algorithm runs.

\begin{lstlisting}
## Q-mode dissimilarity and R-mode correlation on Anderson's iris
iris <- get_data("iris")
Xs   <- scale(iris[, 1:4])          # z-transform (mean 0, sd 1)
D    <- dist(Xs, method = "euclidean")   # 150 x 150 distances (Q view)
R    <- cor(iris[, 1:4])            # 4 x 4 correlations       (R view)
round(R, 2)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=rout]
             Sepal.Length Sepal.Width Petal.Length Petal.Width
Sepal.Length         1.00       -0.12         0.87        0.82
Sepal.Width         -0.12        1.00        -0.43       -0.37
Petal.Length         0.87       -0.43         1.00        0.96
Petal.Width          0.82       -0.37         0.96        1.00
\end{lstlisting}

The correlation block (Figure~\ref{fig:corr}) shows the two petal variables almost
perfectly correlated ($r=0.96$) and sepal width standing apart. In the Q view
(Figure~\ref{fig:diss}), ordering the 150 objects by species reveals a clear block
structure: \emph{setosa} forms a tight, well-separated block, while \emph{versicolor}
and \emph{virginica} bleed into each other — a foreshadowing of every clustering and
classification result later in the book.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering

  \caption{R-mode correlation matrix of the four iris measurements. Petal length and
  width are nearly collinear; sepal width is weakly and negatively related to the rest.}
  \label{fig:corr}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering

\begin{pitfall}
Distances computed on \emph{unstandardized} variables are dominated by whichever
variable happens to have the largest numerical range. Always decide, deliberately,
whether to scale — and remember that Euclidean distance is rarely appropriate for raw
species counts.
\end{pitfall}

\chapter{The Linear Algebra of Ordination}
% ===========================================================================
\section{Transformations as matrices}
Every linear operation on data — rotation, stretching, projection — is a matrix
multiplication $\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{X}$. A symmetric positive-definite matrix
such as a covariance matrix has a \emph{spectral decomposition}
$\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbf{V}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{V}^{\!\top}$, where the columns of
$\mathbf{V}$ (the eigenvectors) are orthogonal directions and the diagonal of
$\mathbf{\Lambda}$ (the eigenvalues) gives the variance along each. Geometrically, the
eigenvectors are the axes of the data's dispersion ellipsoid and the eigenvalues are
the squared semi-axis lengths. This is the engine of PCA.

\section{The singular value decomposition}
The most general and numerically stable move in linear algebra is the \emph{singular value decomposition}
(SVD). Any $n\times p$ matrix factors as
\[
  \mathbf{X}=\mathbf{U}\,\mathbf{D}\,\mathbf{V}^{\!\top},
\]
with $\mathbf{U}$ ($n\times r$) and $\mathbf{V}$ ($p\times r$) containing the left and right singular vectors (both orthonormal), and
$\mathbf{D}$ being the diagonal matrix of singular values $d_1\ge d_2\ge\cdots\ge 0$. 

The remarkable power of SVD lies in its truncation properties. By retaining only the first $k$ terms, we construct
$\mathbf{X}_k=\sum_{i=1}^{k} d_i\,\mathbf{u}_i\mathbf{v}_i^{\!\top}$, which the Eckart--Young--Mirsky theorem guarantees is the absolute best
rank-$k$ approximation of the original matrix $\mathbf{X}$ in the least-squares sense (i.e., minimizing the Frobenius norm of the difference) \cite{Eckart_1936}. Consequently, SVD is simultaneously
the basis of PCA, correspondence analysis, latent semantic analysis, and the low-rank
compression we illustrate next.

\begin{lstlisting}
## Low-rank reconstruction of a grayscale wing image (Limenitis)
G <- get_data("limenitis")          # 69 x 100 intensity matrix
s <- svd(G)
approx <- function(k) s$u[,1:k] %*% diag(s$d[1:k]) %*% t(s$v[,1:k])
err <- sapply(c(1,5,10,50),
              function(k) norm(G - approx(k), "F") / norm(G, "F"))
round(err, 3)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=rout]
[1] 0.197 0.087 0.077 0.020     # relative Frobenius error at rank 1, 5, 10, 50
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{svd_compression.png}
  \caption{SVD compression of a wing image. A rank-1 layer captures the gross shading;
  by rank 5 the structure is essentially recovered, and the singular-value spectrum
  (below) explains why.}
  \label{fig:svdc}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.56\linewidth]{svd_scree.png}
  \caption{Singular-value spectrum (log scale). The rapid decay means a handful of
  components carry most of the matrix's information — the premise of every dimension-
  reduction method in Part II.}
  \label{fig:svds}
\end{figure}

\begin{keybox}
PCA is SVD applied to a centered (and usually scaled) data matrix. Correspondence
analysis is SVD applied to a $\chi^2$-standardized contingency table. Latent semantic
analysis is SVD applied to a term--document matrix. Learn SVD once; recognise it
everywhere.
\end{keybox}

\chapter{Principal Component Analysis}
% ===========================================================================
Principal Component Analysis (PCA), pioneered independently by Karl Pearson \cite{Pearson_1901} and Harold Hotelling \cite{Hotelling_1933}, rotates the variables into a new orthogonal set of axes — the \emph{principal components} — ordered so that the first captures the most variance, the second the most of what remains, and so on. By transforming correlated variables into a smaller number of uncorrelated principal components, PCA effectively separates the signal from the noise in high-dimensional data. Following the revised schedule, this chapter also folds in \emph{Principal Coordinate Analysis} (PCoA), which we show is PCA seen through a distance matrix.

\section{Three routes to the same answer}
PCA can be computed as (i) the spectral decomposition of the correlation matrix
$\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{V}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{V}^{\!\top}$, (ii) the SVD of the scaled
data matrix, or (iii) \R's \code{princomp}/\code{prcomp}. All three return the same
eigenvalues (component variances), loadings (the $\mathbf{V}$ axes), and scores (the
rotated data $\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{X}_s\mathbf{V}$). \code{prcomp} is preferred for
numerical stability because it uses SVD directly.

\begin{lstlisting}
Xs   <- scale(as.matrix(iris[, 1:4]))
R    <- cor(iris[, 1:4])
eig  <- eigen(R)                     # by hand
pc   <- prcomp(iris[, 1:4], scale. = TRUE)
rbind(eigenvalue   = eig$values,
      prop.var     = eig$values / sum(eig$values),
      cumulative   = cumsum(eig$values / sum(eig$values)))
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=rout]
                  PC1      PC2      PC3      PC4
eigenvalue   2.918498 0.914030 0.146757 0.020715
prop.var     0.729624 0.228508 0.036689 0.005179
cumulative   0.729624 0.958132 0.994821 1.000000
\end{lstlisting}

Two components already carry $95.8\%$ of the variance. The equivalence of the routes is
not merely asserted — the code aligns signs and checks
$\max|\,\text{hand scores}-\text{\code{prcomp} scores}\,|\approx 10^{-15}$.

\subsection{Theory: PCA as a variance-maximization problem}
The first principal component is the unit vector $\mathbf{v}_1$ that maximises the
variance of the projected data $\mathbf{X}_s\mathbf{v}$. With $\mathbf{R}$ the
correlation matrix, this is
\[
  \mathbf{v}_1=\arg\max_{\|\mathbf v\|=1}\ \mathbf v^{\!\top}\mathbf R\,\mathbf v .
\]
Introducing a Lagrange multiplier $\lambda$ for the constraint $\mathbf v^{\!\top}\mathbf v=1$
and differentiating $\mathbf v^{\!\top}\mathbf R\mathbf v-\lambda(\mathbf v^{\!\top}\mathbf v-1)$
gives the eigen-equation
\[
  \mathbf R\,\mathbf v=\lambda\,\mathbf v,
\]
so the maximiser is the eigenvector of $\mathbf R$ with the largest eigenvalue, and that
eigenvalue \emph{is} the variance captured, $\mathbf v_1^{\!\top}\mathbf R\mathbf v_1=\lambda_1$.
Each subsequent component maximises the remaining variance subject to orthogonality with
the previous ones, yielding the full eigenbasis $\mathbf R=\mathbf V\mathbf\Lambda\mathbf V^{\!\top}$.
Because $\sum_j\lambda_j=\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf R)=p$ (here $p=4$), the proportion of
variance on component $j$ is simply $\lambda_j/p$ — the numbers in the table above.

\section{How many components? The scree plot}
The \emph{scree plot} graphs eigenvalue against component index; one keeps components
before the ``elbow.'' The \emph{Kaiser rule} keeps components with eigenvalue $>1$
(more variance than a single standardized variable). For iris both rules agree on two.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.56\linewidth]{09_pca_scree.png}
  \caption{Iris PCA scree plot. Only the first two components exceed the Kaiser
  threshold (dashed line at eigenvalue $=1$).}
  \label{fig:pcascree}
\end{figure}

\section{The biplot}
A \emph{biplot} overlays object scores and variable loadings in one picture. Points are
objects in PC space; arrows are variables, their direction showing which components they
drive and their projection onto an axis showing their contribution.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.66\linewidth]{09_pca_biplot.png}
  \caption{Iris PCA biplot. PC1 separates \emph{setosa} (left) from the others and is
  driven by the three positively-correlated size variables; sepal width points downward,
  loading on PC2.}
  \label{fig:pcabi}
\end{figure}

\section{PCoA: PCA through a distance matrix}
Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA), formalized by John Gower \cite{GOWER_1966}, takes a \emph{distance} matrix and finds
a set of Euclidean coordinates whose inter-point distances exactly reproduce the original dissimilarities — this is also known as classical (metric) multidimensional scaling. When the input distance is Euclidean on the same standardized data, PCoA returns the PCA scores exactly. This fundamental equivalence is why the standalone PCoA session is folded here: it is the exact same underlying eigen-problem, generalised to arbitrary non-Euclidean distances (like Gower or Bray--Curtis). This generalisation provides a powerful conceptual bridge, letting us ordinate mixed-type or count data that traditional PCA cannot handle.

\begin{lstlisting}
D    <- dist(scale(iris[, 1:4]))     # Euclidean distance
pcoa <- cmdscale(D, k = 2, eig = TRUE)
cor(pcoa$points[,1], prcomp(iris[,1:4], scale.=TRUE)$x[,1])   # ~ 1.000
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.56\linewidth]{09_pcoa_vs_pca.png}
  \caption{PCoA on a Euclidean distance reproduces the PCA ordination point for point.
  For non-Euclidean distances PCoA generalises PCA to data types PCA cannot handle.}
  \label{fig:pcoa}
\end{figure}

\begin{pitfall}
PCA on the \emph{covariance} matrix lets high-variance variables dominate; on the
\emph{correlation} matrix (scaled data) it treats variables even-handedly. State which
you used — they can give qualitatively different ordinations.
\end{pitfall}

\chapter{Correspondence Analysis}
% ===========================================================================
Correspondence Analysis (CA) is PCA adapted to \emph{counts} — contingency tables of
non-negative integers, such as species abundances across sites. Instead of Euclidean
distance it uses the $\chi^2$ distance, and it ordinates rows (sites) and columns
(species) \emph{simultaneously}, because it is the SVD of a single $\chi^2$-standardized
matrix (recall the Q/R eigenvalue identity from Chapter~1).

\section{The mechanics}
Let $\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{N}/n_{\!++}$ be the table of relative frequencies with row and
column masses $\mathbf{r},\mathbf{c}$. CA takes the SVD of
$\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{D}_r^{-1/2}(\mathbf{P}-\mathbf{r}\mathbf{c}^{\!\top})\mathbf{D}_c^{-1/2}$.
The squared singular values are the \emph{inertias} (variance explained per axis).

The matrix being decomposed is not arbitrary: $\mathbf{P}-\mathbf{r}\mathbf{c}^{\!\top}$ is
precisely the deviation of the observed table from the independence model, and the
$\mathbf{D}_r^{-1/2},\mathbf{D}_c^{-1/2}$ weights convert it into $\chi^2$ units. Total
inertia is therefore the familiar Pearson statistic per observation,
\[
  \text{total inertia}=\sum_{i}\sum_{j}\frac{(p_{ij}-r_i c_j)^2}{r_i c_j}=\frac{\chi^{2}}{n_{++}},
\]
so CA is, exactly, a decomposition of the $\chi^2$ statistic into orthogonal axes. The
distance it preserves among rows is the $\chi^2$ distance
\[
  d^2_{\chi^2}(i,i')=\sum_{j}\frac{1}{c_j}\left(\frac{p_{ij}}{r_i}-\frac{p_{i'j}}{r_{i'}}\right)^{2},
\]
which compares row \emph{profiles} rather than raw counts and down-weights abundant columns
by dividing through by their mass. That is what makes CA appropriate for species-by-site
tables where PCA is not: two sites with the same relative composition are placed together
regardless of total abundance, and rare species are not automatically drowned out.

An equivalent and more intuitive characterisation is \emph{reciprocal averaging}: assign
arbitrary scores to the sites, compute each species' score as the weighted average of the
sites in which it occurs, then recompute each site's score as the weighted average of its
species, and iterate. Rescaled at each step, this procedure converges to the first CA axis
— the same eigenvector the SVD returns. It also explains the method's characteristic
behaviour, since sites and species are being placed on a single shared gradient by
construction.

\begin{lstlisting}
library(vegan)
fish <- get_data("doubs")$fish      # 30 sites x 27 fish species (counts)
ca   <- cca(fish)                    # correspondence analysis in vegan

\chapter{Multidimensional Scaling}
% ===========================================================================
Where PCA and CA are eigen-methods, \emph{non-metric multidimensional scaling} (nMDS)
is an \emph{optimisation}. It seeks a low-dimensional configuration whose inter-point
distances preserve the \emph{rank order} of the original dissimilarities, minimising a
badness-of-fit criterion called \emph{stress}. It makes no distributional assumptions and
accepts any dissimilarity, which makes it the most flexible ordination in the toolbox
\cite{Legendre_2012}.

\section{Stress, and what is actually being fitted}
Let $d_{ij}$ be the observed dissimilarity between objects $i$ and $j$, and $\delta_{ij}$
the Euclidean distance between their positions in the low-dimensional configuration. nMDS
minimises Kruskal's stress
\[
  S=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i<j}\left(\delta_{ij}-\hat{\delta}_{ij}\right)^{2}}
                {\sum_{i<j}\delta_{ij}^{2}}},
\]
where the $\hat{\delta}_{ij}$ are \emph{disparities}: the fitted values of a monotone
regression of $\delta$ on $d$. This is the crux of the method. The disparities are required
only to preserve the ordering of the original dissimilarities — if $d_{ij}<d_{kl}$ then
$\hat{\delta}_{ij}\le\hat{\delta}_{kl}$ — and are otherwise free. So nMDS never asks the map
to reproduce the dissimilarities themselves, only their rank order, which is exactly what
makes it usable with ordinal or highly non-linear dissimilarity measures.

Fitting alternates two steps until convergence: given the configuration, obtain the
disparities by isotonic regression; given the disparities, move the points to reduce $S$ by
gradient descent. Both steps decrease the same objective, but the surface is not convex,
which is why \code{metaMDS} restarts the optimisation from several random configurations
and keeps the best — a solution reached once may be a local minimum.

The Shepard diagram plots $\delta_{ij}$ against $d_{ij}$ with the fitted monotone step
function overlaid; it is the direct visual audit of the assumption the method makes.

\begin{lstlisting}
library(vegan)
md <- metaMDS(data, distance = "bray", k = 2)   # random-start optimisation
md$stress                                        # < 0.1 good, < 0.2 usable
stressplot(md)                                   # Shepard diagram
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{15_mds.png}
  \caption{Left: an nMDS configuration. Right: the Shepard diagram plots configuration
  distance against original distance; a tight, monotone increasing cloud (near the red
  line) means the low-dimensional map faithfully preserves the dissimilarity ranks.}
  \label{fig:mds}
\end{figure}

\begin{keybox}
Stress is an optimisation score, not a probability. A low stress means the picture is a
faithful map of the distances; it does \emph{not} certify that the distance you chose was
the right question. Garbage distance in, low-stress garbage out.
\end{keybox}

\chapter{Non-negative Matrix Factorization}
% ===========================================================================
Non-negative Matrix Factorization (NMF) approximates a non-negative data matrix as
$\mathbf{A}\approx\mathbf{W}\mathbf{H}$ with \emph{both} factors non-negative
\cite{Lee_1999}. Because nothing can cancel, the columns of $\mathbf{W}$ act as additive
``parts'' (metagenes, motifs, topics) and the columns of $\mathbf{H}$ are the amounts of
each part in each sample. The non-negativity buys interpretability that PCA's signed
loadings lack.

\section{The factorization and how it is found}
For an $n\times p$ matrix $\mathbf{A}\ge 0$ and a chosen rank $k\ll\min(n,p)$, NMF solves
\[
  \min_{\mathbf{W}\ge 0,\ \mathbf{H}\ge 0}\ \lVert \mathbf{A}-\mathbf{W}\mathbf{H}\rVert_F^{2},
  \qquad \mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k},\ \mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times p}.
\]
Unlike the SVD of Chapter~2, this problem has no closed-form solution and is not convex in
$\mathbf{W}$ and $\mathbf{H}$ jointly — although it \emph{is} convex in each factor with the
other held fixed, which is what makes alternating schemes attractive. The standard
multiplicative update rules exploit exactly that structure:
\[
  H_{a\mu}\leftarrow H_{a\mu}\,\frac{(\mathbf{W}^{\!\top}\mathbf{A})_{a\mu}}{(\mathbf{W}^{\!\top}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{H})_{a\mu}},
  \qquad
  W_{ia}\leftarrow W_{ia}\,\frac{(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{H}^{\!\top})_{ia}}{(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{\!\top})_{ia}} .
\]
Because every quantity on the right is non-negative, the updates preserve non-negativity
automatically — no projection step is needed — and they do not increase the reconstruction
error.

Two consequences matter in practice. First, the solution is only identified up to a
diagonal rescaling ($\mathbf{W}\mathbf{D}$ and $\mathbf{D}^{-1}\mathbf{H}$ fit equally well),
so the absolute magnitudes of the loadings should not be over-interpreted. Second, because
the objective is non-convex, different starting points give different answers; the
\code{nndsvd} initialisation used below is a deterministic SVD-based seed chosen precisely
to make runs reproducible.

\begin{keybox}
PCA and NMF factor the same matrix under different constraints, and the constraint is the
whole story. PCA imposes orthogonality and admits negative loadings, so components cancel
and represent \emph{contrasts}. NMF forbids cancellation, so factors can only add, and the
representation becomes \emph{parts} that sum to the whole. This is why NMF factors of gene
expression read as co-expressed modules while PCA components read as axes of variation.
\end{keybox}

\begin{lstlisting}
library(NMF)
expr <- get_data("leukemia")         # 1000 genes x 60 patients
res  <- nmf(expr - min(expr), rank = 3, seed = "nndsvd")
basismap(res); coefmap(res)          # W (gene signatures) and H (sample loadings)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{pitfall}
The rank $k$ is a modelling choice, not an output, and choosing it below the number of
groups actually present guarantees that some groups will be merged. A rank-3 factorization
of a cohort spanning five clinical subtypes can at best isolate a few of them and must
collapse the rest — an artefact of the chosen rank rather than evidence that the merged
subtypes are biologically alike. Fit a range of ranks and inspect the cophenetic
correlation or residual error before settling on one.
\end{pitfall}

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{17_nmf.png}
  \caption{Rank-3 NMF of a leukemia expression matrix. Left: the basis $\mathbf{W}$
  (gene signatures). Right: the coefficients $\mathbf{H}$, with samples ordered by
  subtype — the factors recover group structure without ever seeing the labels.}
  \label{fig:nmf}
\end{figure}

\chapter{Exploratory Factor Analysis}
% ===========================================================================
Factor analysis (FA) looks superficially like PCA but rests on a different model. PCA
re-expresses \emph{all} the variance with components that are exact linear combinations
of the variables. FA posits a small number of unobserved \emph{common factors} that
generate the correlations among variables, and it explicitly separates shared variance
from variable-specific noise.

\section{The model}
With $p$ standardized variables and $k<p$ latent factors $\mathbf f$,
\[
  \mathbf x=\mathbf\Lambda\,\mathbf f+\boldsymbol\varepsilon,\qquad
  \operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf x)=\mathbf\Lambda\mathbf\Lambda^{\!\top}+\mathbf\Psi,
\]
where $\mathbf\Lambda$ ($p\times k$) holds the \emph{loadings}, $\mathbf f$ are the
common factors (uncorrelated, unit variance), and $\mathbf\Psi$ is a diagonal matrix of
\emph{uniquenesses} — the part of each variable not explained by the common factors. The
key contrast with PCA is that term $\mathbf\Psi$: FA does not try to fit variable-specific
variance, only the \emph{common} structure. Estimation is by maximum likelihood, and the
loadings are then \emph{rotated} (e.g.\ varimax) to a more interpretable, near-simple
structure.

\begin{lstlisting}
X  <- scale(get_data("taxon")[, -1])
fa <- factanal(X, factors = 2, rotation = "varimax")
round(eigen(cor(X))$values, 3)          # how many factors?
print(fa$loadings, digits = 2, cutoff = 0.2)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=rout]

Three eigenvalues exceed one, so the Kaiser rule would retain three factors, while the
model fitted here requests two. The two criteria need not agree, and the likelihood-ratio
test adjudicates: at $p=0.11$ it does not reject the two-factor model, so two factors are a
defensible simplification even though a third eigenvalue clears the threshold.

The uniquenesses are the more revealing output. \code{Petiole} and \code{Leaf} have
uniquenesses at essentially zero — the common factors explain them almost entirely — while
\code{Internode} and \code{Bract} sit at $0.99$ and $0.98$, meaning they are almost pure
variable-specific variance and share nothing with the rest. A variable like that
contributes nothing to a common-factor model, and its near-unit uniqueness is the
diagnostic that says so. PCA, which has no $\mathbf{\Psi}$ term, cannot make this
distinction at all: it would simply hand \code{Internode} a loading and leave the reader to
guess whether it meant anything.

\begin{pitfall}
A uniqueness that is exactly $0$ (a \emph{Heywood case}) or exactly $1$ signals a boundary
solution, and the corresponding loading should not be read as a precise estimate.
Maximum-likelihood factor analysis is constrained to $\mathbf{\Psi}\ge0$, and when the
unconstrained optimum lies outside that region the estimate is pinned to the boundary.
\end{pitfall}

  \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{16_fa_scree.png}

  \label{fig:fa}
\end{figure}

\begin{pitfall}
PCA and FA are not interchangeable. Use PCA to \emph{reduce} dimensionality (summarise
total variance); use FA to \emph{explain} correlations by latent constructs. Reporting
PCA loadings as ``factors'' — a common slip — conflates the two models.
\end{pitfall}

% ===========================================================================
\chapter{Clustering}
% ===========================================================================
Clustering partitions objects into groups without using labels. We cover the two
classroom workhorses: \emph{k-means} (partition around $k$ centroids) and
\emph{hierarchical} clustering (a nested tree, or dendrogram).

\section{k-means and the elbow}
k-means minimises the total within-cluster sum of squares (WSS). Writing $C_1,\dots,C_k$
for the partition and $\bar{\mathbf{x}}_m$ for the centroid of cluster $m$, the objective is
\[
  W(C)=\sum_{m=1}^{k}\ \sum_{i\in C_m}\lVert \mathbf{x}_i-\bar{\mathbf{x}}_m\rVert^{2}.
\]
Minimising this exactly over all partitions is combinatorially infeasible, so Lloyd's
algorithm alternates two steps — assign each point to its nearest centroid, then recompute
each centroid as the mean of its members — each of which can only decrease $W(C)$. The
procedure therefore converges, but to a local minimum that depends on the initial centroids;
running it repeatedly from different random starts (\code{nstart}) and keeping the best is
not optional book-keeping but part of the method.

Two properties follow from the objective and are worth stating plainly. Because $W(C)$ uses
squared Euclidean distance to a mean, k-means implicitly looks for compact, roughly
spherical, similarly-sized clusters, and will impose that shape whether or not the data have
it. And because $W(C)$ decreases monotonically with $k$ — more centroids can never fit worse
— its value cannot be used to choose $k$. Hence the \emph{elbow}: we look for the point where
added clusters stop paying off, rather than for a minimum that does not exist.

\begin{lstlisting}
w <- wss(as.matrix(iris[,1:4]))      # WSS for k = 1..10 (helper in 05_Clustering.R)
plot(w, type = "b"); abline(v = 3, lty = 2)
km3 <- kmeans(iris[,1:4], 3, nstart = 25)
table(cluster = km3$cluster, species = iris$Species)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=rout]
        species
cluster  setosa versicolor virginica
      1       0         48        14
      2       0          2        36
      3      50          0         0

Agreement (max assignment) = 0.893
\end{lstlisting}

The elbow sits at $k=3$ (Figure~\ref{fig:elbow}). The cross-tabulation shows the
unsupervised clusters recover the species with $89.3\%$ agreement: \emph{setosa} is
perfect — it occupies a cluster of its own, and nothing else is assigned to it — while
\emph{versicolor} and \emph{virginica} bleed into each other across two clusters, exactly
the structure the Q-mode heatmap warned about in Chapter~1. Note that the cluster
\emph{numbers} carry no meaning: k-means never sees the labels, so which partition is
called ``1'' is an artefact of initialisation, and only the grouping is interpretable.

  \includegraphics[width=0.52\linewidth]{05_kmeans_elbow.png}\hfill
  \includegraphics[width=0.44\linewidth]{05_kmeans_pairs.png}
  \caption{Left: k-means elbow — WSS drops sharply to $k=3$ then flattens. Right: pairwise
  scatterplots coloured by cluster assignment; the \emph{setosa} cluster separates cleanly
  on every pair involving petal measurements, while the other two overlap along the
  versicolor/virginica boundary — 16 of 150 objects in total.}
  \label{fig:elbow}
\end{figure}

\section{Hierarchical clustering and linkage}
Agglomerative clustering repeatedly merges the two closest groups. The \emph{linkage}
rule defines ``closest'', and the choice is substantive rather than cosmetic. For clusters
$A$ and $B$,
\[
  D_{\text{single}}(A,B)=\min_{i\in A,\,j\in B} d_{ij},
  \qquad
  D_{\text{complete}}(A,B)=\max_{i\in A,\,j\in B} d_{ij},
\]
\[
  D_{\text{average}}(A,B)=\frac{1}{|A||B|}\sum_{i\in A}\sum_{j\in B} d_{ij}
  \quad\text{(UPGMA)},
\]
while Ward's criterion merges the pair that produces the smallest increase in total
within-cluster sum of squares,
\[
  \Delta(A,B)=\frac{|A|\,|B|}{|A|+|B|}\,\lVert \bar{\mathbf{x}}_A-\bar{\mathbf{x}}_B\rVert^{2}.
\]
Ward is thus the agglomerative counterpart of the k-means objective above, which is why the
two often agree, and why Ward — like k-means — favours compact, comparably-sized clusters.
Single linkage sits at the opposite extreme: because only the nearest pair of points
matters, it can chain elongated groups together through a bridge of intermediates, which is
a liability when clusters are meant to be compact and an asset when they genuinely are
elongated. The dendrogram makes the whole nested sequence visible at once, but the choice of
where to cut it remains an external decision, exactly as the choice of $k$ does for k-means.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{hclust_dendrogram.png}
  \caption{Ward dendrogram of world regions clustered on standardized biodiversity
  variables. Cutting the tree at a chosen height yields discrete groups.}
  \label{fig:dendro}
\end{figure}

\begin{pitfall}
k-means depends on the random initial centroids; always use \code{nstart} $>1$ (we use
25) so the reported solution is the best of many restarts, not a lucky or unlucky one.
Different linkages can produce very different trees from the \emph{same} distances — the
choice is substantive, not cosmetic.
\end{pitfall}

\chapter{Discriminant Analysis}
% ===========================================================================
Discriminant analysis is \emph{supervised} ordination: given labelled groups, it finds
the axes that best \emph{separate} them. Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) maximises the
ratio of between-group to within-group variance, assuming equal group covariances;
Quadratic DA (QDA) relaxes that assumption.

\subsection{Theory: Fisher's criterion}
Let $\mathbf B$ be the between-group and $\mathbf W$ the within-group scatter matrix.
Fisher sought the direction $\mathbf a$ maximising the separation ratio
\[
  J(\mathbf a)=\frac{\mathbf a^{\!\top}\mathbf B\,\mathbf a}{\mathbf a^{\!\top}\mathbf W\,\mathbf a}.
\]
Setting $\partial J/\partial\mathbf a=\mathbf 0$ yields the \emph{generalized}
eigenproblem $\mathbf B\mathbf a=\ell\,\mathbf W\mathbf a$, i.e.\ the ordinary eigenproblem
$\mathbf W^{-1}\mathbf B\,\mathbf a=\ell\,\mathbf a$. The leading eigenvectors are the
discriminant axes and their eigenvalues (the ``proportion of trace'') say how much group
separation each axis carries. With $g$ groups there are at most $g-1$ such axes — here,
three for four taxa.

\begin{lstlisting}
library(MASS)
model <- lda(Taxon ~ ., data = train)      # taxon: 4 morphometric groups
pred  <- predict(model, test)$class
mean(pred == test$Taxon)                    # hold-out accuracy
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=rout]
[1] 1.000       # perfect separation on held-out data (well-separated groups)
\end{lstlisting}

Figure~\ref{fig:lda} makes the point of supervision vivid: PCA, which never sees the
labels, smears the four groups along directions of raw variance; LDA, which uses them,
opens clean gaps. That gain is real when groups differ, and illusory when they do not —
hence the assumption checks below.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{lda_vs_pca.png}
  \caption{The same four-group data under PCA (left, unsupervised) and LDA (right,
  supervised). LDA's axes are built to separate the known classes.}
  \label{fig:lda}
\end{figure}

\begin{pitfall}
LDA assumes multivariate normality and \emph{equal covariance matrices} across groups.
Test the second assumption (e.g.\ \code{vegan::betadisper} with a permutation test)
before trusting an LDA; when it fails, prefer QDA or a tree-based classifier from the
next chapter.
\end{pitfall}

\chapter{Multivariate Generalized Linear Models}
% ===========================================================================
Ordinary regression assumes a continuous, unbounded response with Gaussian, constant-
variance errors. Ecological responses rarely oblige: biomass is positive, counts are
integers, presence is binary. \emph{Generalized linear models} (GLMs) keep the linear
predictor $\eta=\mathbf x^{\!\top}\boldsymbol\beta$ but connect it to the mean through a
\emph{link} function and allow a non-Gaussian error \emph{family}.

\section{Link and family}
A GLM has three parts: a linear predictor $\eta$; a link $g$ with $g(\mu)=\eta$; and an
exponential-family distribution for the response. Choose the family by the response type
— Gamma (log link) for positive continuous data, Poisson (log link) for counts, binomial
(logit link) for 0/1 — and fit by iteratively reweighted least squares.

\begin{lstlisting}
m <- get_data("crawley")            # mixed-type ecological table
shapiro.test(m$Biomass)$p.value     # small p => Gaussian OLS is wrong
g_gamma <- glm(Biomass ~ Tmp + pH, family = Gamma(link = "log"), data = m)
g_pois  <- glm(Species ~ Tmp + pH, family = poisson,             data = m)
AIC(g_pois, glm(Species ~ Tmp * pH, family = poisson, data = m))  # additive vs interaction
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=rout]

errors will be too small and everything looks significant.
\end{pitfall}

\chapter{Canonical Correlation and Redundancy Analysis}
% ===========================================================================
The final classical methods generalise correlation and regression from vectors to
\emph{two tables}. Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCorA) is the two-table analogue of
correlation — symmetric, no direction. Redundancy Analysis (RDA) is the two-table
analogue of regression — directional, one table explained by the other.

\section{Canonical correlation}
Given tables $\mathbf X$ ($n\times p$) and $\mathbf Y$ ($n\times q$), CCorA finds pairs of
linear combinations $\mathbf X\mathbf a$ and $\mathbf Y\mathbf b$ with maximal
correlation, then a second orthogonal pair, and so on. The canonical correlations are the
square roots of the eigenvalues of
$\mathbf R_{xx}^{-1}\mathbf R_{xy}\mathbf R_{yy}^{-1}\mathbf R_{yx}$, and Pillai's trace
(with a permutation test) assesses whether the two tables are associated at all.

\begin{lstlisting}
doubs <- get_data("doubs")
Y <- decostand(doubs$fish, "hellinger")    # response table (fish)
X <- scale(doubs$env)                       # predictor table (environment)
CCorA(scale(doubs$fish), X)$p.perm          # are the tables associated?
rda.mod <- rda(Y ~ ., data = as.data.frame(X))   # directional: fish ~ environment
RsquareAdj(rda.mod)$adj.r.squared
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Redundancy analysis}
RDA regresses \emph{every} response variable on the predictors, collects the fitted
values into a matrix, and runs a PCA on that fitted matrix. The variance thus splits into
a \emph{constrained} part (explained by $\mathbf X$) and an \emph{unconstrained} part
(residual), and an \emph{adjusted} $R^2$ corrects the optimistic raw fraction for the
number of predictors. A permutation test (\code{anova.cca}) provides overall
significance, and \code{ordiR2step} does forward selection of predictors.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.66\linewidth]{20_rda.png}
  \caption{RDA triplot: sites (points), species (text) and environmental predictors
  (arrows). Arrow length and direction show which gradients drive community composition;
  this is multivariate multiple regression made visual.}
  \label{fig:rda}
\end{figure}

\begin{keybox}
Canonical correlation asks ``are these two tables related?'' (symmetric); redundancy
analysis asks ``how much of table $\mathbf Y$ does table $\mathbf X$ explain?''
(directional). RDA is the multivariate regression that closes the classical half of the
course — and it partitions variance exactly as an ANOVA does, but for a whole community
at once.
\end{keybox}

\chapter{Supervised Machine Learning}
% ===========================================================================
The classical classifiers of Chapter~8 assume a functional form and estimate its
parameters. Tree-based learners abandon that premise entirely: they \emph{partition}
the feature space with axis-aligned splits, capturing interactions and non-linearities
without any distributional assumption. This chapter follows two worked ecological
problems — discriminating anuran species from the acoustic structure of their calls,
and predicting avian trophic niche from morphology — through the full ladder from a
single tree to tuned ensembles.

\section{Recursive partitioning}
A classification tree is grown greedily. At each node the algorithm searches every
variable $j$ and every candidate split point $s$, and chooses the pair that most reduces
an \emph{impurity} measure. For a node $t$ holding class proportions $p_{1t},\dots,p_{Kt}$,
the Gini index and the entropy are
\[
  i_{\text{Gini}}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{kt}(1-p_{kt}),
  \qquad
  i_{\text{ent}}(t)=-\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{kt}\log p_{kt},
\]
and the split is chosen to maximise the decrease
$\Delta i = i(t) - \pi_L\, i(t_L) - \pi_R\, i(t_R)$, where $\pi_L,\pi_R$ are the fractions
of the node's observations sent left and right. Both measures are maximised by a
50/50 mixture and vanish for a pure node, so both reward splits that separate classes.

Grown unchecked, this procedure will isolate every observation and memorise the training
set. The standard remedy is \emph{cost-complexity pruning}: for a tree $T$ with $|T|$
terminal nodes and misclassification cost $R(T)$, define
\[
  R_\alpha(T) = R(T) + \alpha\,|T| ,
\]
and, for each $\alpha$, keep the subtree minimising $R_\alpha$. The complexity parameter
$\alpha$ (\code{cp} in \pkg{rpart}) buys simplicity at a price in fit. Rather than take the
$\alpha$ with the lowest cross-validated error outright, it is safer to apply the
\emph{1-SE rule}: choose the smallest tree whose cross-validated error lies within one
standard error of the minimum. That is what the pruning helper in \code{UnitCART} does.

\begin{lstlisting}
prune_model <- function(model, data) {
  cp_table <- printcp(model)
  best_cp_index <- which.min(cp_table[, "xerror"])
  ## walk back up the table while the simpler tree is still within 1 SE
  while (cp_table[, "xerror"][best_cp_index - 1] <
         cp_table[, "xerror"][best_cp_index] + cp_table[, "xstd"][best_cp_index]) {
    best_cp_index <- best_cp_index - 1
  }
  prune(model, cp = cp_table[, "CP"][best_cp_index])
}
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Case study: anuran calls}
The first data set contains 7195 recordings of frog calls, each summarised by 22
Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs), labelled at three taxonomic levels: 4
families, 8 genera and 10 species. It is a genuinely multivariate problem, and a sharply
imbalanced one — \emph{Leptodactylidae} contributes 4420 recordings while
\emph{Bufonidae} contributes only 68.

Before any model is fitted, the 22 predictors are screened for redundancy. This is the
R-mode correlation view of Chapter~1 doing practical work: one variable from every pair
correlated above $|r|=0.7$ is dropped, since collinear predictors add no information and
make the resulting tree harder to read.

\begin{lstlisting}
AnuranData_cor <- AnuranData |> select(-Family, -Genus, -Species) |> correlate()
working_variables <- trim_correlated(AnuranData_cor, threshold = 0.7)
AnuranData <- AnuranData |> select(Family, Genus, Species, working_variables$var)

## stratified split preserves species proportions -- and therefore genus and family too
split   <- initial_split(AnuranData, prop = .75, strata = Species)
Training <- training(split); Testing <- testing(split)
\end{lstlisting}

Stratifying on species is a deliberate choice: because species nest within genera and
genera within families, preserving species proportions automatically preserves the
proportions at both higher levels.

\subsection{The ensemble ladder}
A single tree is interpretable but high-variance — refit it on a slightly different
sample and the splits move. Three ideas tame that variance, and the unit fits all of them.

\emph{Bagging} \cite{Breiman_1996} averages trees grown on bootstrap resamples. Averaging
$B$ identically distributed trees each with variance $\sigma^2$ and pairwise correlation
$\rho$ yields a predictor with variance
\[
  \rho\sigma^{2}+\frac{1-\rho}{B}\,\sigma^{2},
\]
so the second term vanishes as $B$ grows but the first does not: correlation between the
trees sets a floor on how much averaging can help.

The \emph{random forest} \cite{Breiman_2001} attacks precisely that floor by sampling
\code{mtry} of the predictors at each split, which de-correlates the trees and pushes
$\rho$ down. Its established value in ecology \cite{Cutler_2007} rests on exactly this
property, together with the out-of-bag error estimate that comes free with bootstrapping.

\emph{Boosting} takes the opposite tack: instead of averaging independent trees, it grows
them sequentially, each one fitting what its predecessors got wrong. AdaBoost
\cite{Freund_1997} does this by reweighting misclassified observations; gradient boosting
\cite{Friedman_2001} generalises it to descent on an arbitrary differentiable loss, and
XGBoost \cite{Chen_2016} adds explicit regularisation and second-order information.

\begin{lstlisting}
bagging_model_Species <- ipred::bagging(Species ~ ., data = Training,
                                        coob = TRUE, nbagg = 300,
                                        control = rpart.control(minsplit = 2, cp = 0))

rf_model_Species <- randomForest(Species ~ ., data = Training,
                                 ntree = 1000, mtry = 5, importance = TRUE)

ada_model_Species <- boosting(Species ~ ., data = Training,
                              control = rpart.control(maxdepth = 5))

xgb_model_Species <- xgboost(data = xgb_matrix_Species, nrounds = 200,
                             params = list(eta = 0.01, max_depth = 7,
                                           min_child_weight = 3,
                                           subsample = 0.5, colsample_bytree = 0.5),
                             objective = "multi:softmax", num_class = 10)
\end{lstlisting}

Every model is compared under the same stratified 10-fold cross-validation, implemented
as an S3 generic (\code{manual\_cv}) with methods for \code{rpart}, \code{classbagg},
\code{randomForest} and \code{boosting} objects, so that the resampling scheme — not the
package's internal defaults — is held constant across the comparison.

Ordering the out-of-sample estimates reproduces the textbook hierarchy: unpruned trees
overfit badly, pruning recovers most of the loss, bagging improves on a single tree, the
forest improves on bagging, and boosting wins. Adaptive boosting carried to the held-out
quarter of the recordings classifies species with an accuracy of $0.90$.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=rout]
performance_test <- get_metrics(ada_model_Species, data = Testing)
#  held-out accuracy: 0.90   (10 species, 25% of 7195 recordings)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{pitfall}
Accuracy is a poor summary under class imbalance. A classifier that never predicts
\emph{Bufonidae} at all forfeits only 68 of 7195 recordings — under $1\%$ — and still
looks excellent. Report the confusion matrix, and prefer Cohen's $\kappa$ or per-class
recall when prevalence is this skewed.
\end{pitfall}

\section{Case study: AVONET, and one interface for everything}
The second problem uses AVONET \cite{Tobias_2022}, a morphological, ecological and
geographical compilation covering all birds; the working table here holds 11\,009 species
described by beak, tarsus, wing, tail and mass measurements. Two questions are asked of
the same predictors: a \emph{classification} — which trophic niche does a species occupy?
— and a \emph{regression} — how large is its geographic range?

Comparing a dozen model families by hand is where \pkg{caret} \cite{Kuhn_2008} earns its
place. One \code{trainControl} object fixes the resampling scheme; one \code{train} call
per method handles tuning-grid construction, fitting and evaluation; and \code{resamples}
compares the results on identical folds.

\begin{lstlisting}
ctrl <- trainControl(method = "repeatedcv", number = 10, repeats = 3)
models <- list(
  rf        = list(method = "rf"),          # random forest
  gbm       = list(method = "gbm"),         # gradient boosting
  svmRadial = list(method = "svmRadial"),   # SVM, radial basis kernel
  earth     = list(method = "earth"),       # MARS
  MANN      = list(method = "avNNet"),      # model-averaged neural network
  HDA       = list(method = "hda"))         # heteroscedastic discriminant analysis
\end{lstlisting}

Note what the candidate list contains: \code{hda} is a discriminant analysis — the method
of Chapter~8 — entered as one competitor among many, and \code{avNNet} is a small neural
network, the subject of Chapter~14. The framework is indifferent to a method's pedigree;
it asks only how each performs under a common resampling protocol.

For the trophic-niche classification the random forest won, reaching an accuracy of
$0.79$ and $\kappa=0.64$ on the held-out data after tuning \code{mtry} over an expanded
grid. For range-size regression the winner was MARS (\pkg{earth}), tuned over the degree
of interaction and the number of retained terms.

\begin{keybox}
The workflow, not the algorithm, is the lesson: split the data, resample honestly, tune
on the training folds only, and report on a held-out test set. Because $\kappa$ corrects
for agreement expected by chance, the gap between accuracy $0.79$ and $\kappa=0.64$ is
itself informative — it is the part of the apparent performance that the class
frequencies alone would have bought.
\end{keybox}

\chapter{Unsupervised Machine Learning: t-SNE and UMAP}
% ===========================================================================
PCA is a \emph{linear} projection: it preserves global variance but can leave curved,
clustered structure tangled. Modern \emph{manifold learners} — t-SNE and UMAP — are
non-linear and optimise for preserving \emph{local} neighbourhoods, so they pull apart
clusters that PCA overlaps. They are the unsupervised counterpart to the ordinations of
Part II, and the standard first look at high-dimensional omics data.

\section{From distances to probabilities}
t-SNE \cite{vanderMaaten_2008} does not try to reproduce distances at all; it reproduces
\emph{neighbourhood probabilities}. In the high-dimensional space it converts distances
into conditional probabilities with a Gaussian kernel centred on each point,
\[
  p_{j|i}=\frac{\exp\!\left(-\lVert \mathbf{x}_i-\mathbf{x}_j\rVert^{2}/2\sigma_i^{2}\right)}
               {\sum_{k\neq i}\exp\!\left(-\lVert \mathbf{x}_i-\mathbf{x}_k\rVert^{2}/2\sigma_i^{2}\right)},
\]
where each bandwidth $\sigma_i$ is set so that the entropy of $p_{\cdot|i}$ matches a
user-chosen \emph{perplexity} — effectively, the number of neighbours each point is asked
to care about. In the low-dimensional map the corresponding affinities $q_{ij}$ use a
Student-$t$ kernel with one degree of freedom, and the layout minimises the
Kullback--Leibler divergence
\[
  \mathrm{KL}(P\,\Vert\,Q)=\sum_{i\neq j}p_{ij}\log\frac{p_{ij}}{q_{ij}} .
\]
Two consequences follow directly from that objective. The heavy-tailed $t$ kernel is a
deliberate fix for the \emph{crowding problem}: in two dimensions there is simply less room
than in a thousand, and without heavy tails moderately distant points would all be crushed
together. And because the divergence is asymmetric, placing a large $q$ where $p$ is small
costs little — which is exactly why \emph{between}-cluster distances in a t-SNE plot carry
almost no information.

UMAP \cite{McInnes_2018} reaches a similar place from a different direction. It builds a
weighted $k$-nearest-neighbour graph, treats it as a fuzzy topological representation of
the data, and then optimises a low-dimensional graph to match it via cross-entropy. In
practice it is faster than t-SNE, and it tends to retain somewhat more of the global
arrangement, though neither method should be read as a map of true distances.

\begin{lstlisting}
library(Rtsne); library(uwot)
X  <- t(get_data("leukemia"))         # 60 patients x 1000 genes, 5 subtypes
pc <- prcomp(X, scale. = TRUE)
ts <- Rtsne(X, perplexity = 15)$Y     # t-SNE
um <- umap(X, n_neighbors = 15)       # UMAP
round(pc$sdev^2 / sum(pc$sdev^2), 3)[1:3]
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=rout]
PCA variance explained (PC1-3): 0.324 0.149 0.071
\end{lstlisting}

That PC1 accounts for under a third of the variance is itself the argument for the other
two methods. With the linear projection spreading the signal thinly across many components,
a two-dimensional PCA view is necessarily a partial one; the manifold learners spend their
two dimensions on local structure instead.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{26_embeddings.png}
  \caption{Three views of the same leukemia expression matrix (1000 genes, 60 patients).
  PCA (left) is the linear baseline; t-SNE (middle) and UMAP (right) reorganise the same
  samples by local neighbourhood structure. Cluster \emph{positions} and \emph{separations}
  in the two right-hand panels are not to be read quantitatively.}
  \label{fig:embed}
\end{figure}

\begin{pitfall}
Quantitative summaries computed on top of an embedding inherit whatever labelling the
script assigns. If sample identifiers are parsed with the wrong delimiter — splitting
\code{ALL\_GSM330151} on a period rather than an underscore, say — every sample ends up in
a class of its own, and neighbourhood-purity scores collapse to zero for all three methods
simultaneously. A metric that is identically zero across methods that plainly differ in the
figure is a bug in the labels, not a finding about the embeddings.
\end{pitfall}

\begin{pitfall}
t-SNE and UMAP distort \emph{global} geometry: distances \emph{between} well-separated
clusters, and cluster \emph{sizes}, are not meaningful, and the layout depends on
perplexity / \code{n\_neighbors}. Use them to \emph{see} groups, not to measure how far
apart groups are — for that, return to PCA or PCoA.
\end{pitfall}

\chapter{Neural Networks and Deep Learning}
% ===========================================================================
A neural network stacks linear maps and non-linear activations so that, with enough
hidden units, it can approximate essentially any function; training adjusts the weights by
gradient descent to minimise a loss \cite{LeCun_2015}. This chapter runs in two tracks: a
small network that executes anywhere, and the full deep-learning treatment of three
biological problems.

\section{What the network computes}
A dense feed-forward network of $L$ layers maps an input $\mathbf{x}$ to an output by
alternating affine transformations with a non-linearity $\sigma$:
\[
  \mathbf{h}^{(0)}=\mathbf{x},\qquad
  \mathbf{h}^{(\ell)}=\sigma\!\left(\mathbf{W}^{(\ell)}\mathbf{h}^{(\ell-1)}+\mathbf{b}^{(\ell)}\right),
  \qquad \ell=1,\dots,L .
\]
Strip out $\sigma$ and the whole stack collapses: a composition of affine maps is itself
affine, and the network could do no more than the linear methods of Part~II. The
non-linearity is what buys the extra expressive power, and with a single sufficiently wide
hidden layer the family is already a universal approximator.

Weights are fitted by gradient descent on a loss $\mathcal{L}$, with the gradients obtained
by applying the chain rule backwards through the layers (backpropagation). For
classification the loss is the categorical cross-entropy
\[
  \mathcal{L}=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K} y_{ik}\log \hat{p}_{ik},
\]
which is exactly the negative log-likelihood of a multinomial model — the same estimation
principle as the GLMs of Chapter~11, applied to a far more flexible mean function.

\section{Track A: a network you can run anywhere}
The self-contained track uses base-\R's \pkg{nnet}: one hidden layer, softmax output,
weight decay for regularisation. It needs no Python, keras or GPU, so it always runs.

\begin{lstlisting}
library(nnet)
Xs  <- scale(as.matrix(iris[,1:4]))
Y   <- class.ind(iris$Species)              # one-hot targets
net <- nnet(Xs[idx,], Y[idx,], size = 8, softmax = TRUE, decay = 5e-3, maxit = 400)
mean(predict(net, Xs[-idx,], type = "class") == iris$Species[-idx])
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=rout]
nnet test accuracy: 0.956
Network architecture: 4 inputs -> 8 hidden (logistic) -> 3 softmax; 67 weights total.
\end{lstlisting}

The weight-decay term is not decoration. With 67 weights fitted to 105 training rows the
network has ample capacity to memorise; the penalty $\lambda\sum w^2$ added to the loss is
what keeps it from doing so, and is the direct analogue of ridge shrinkage.

\section{Track B: three problems in biology}
The full track uses \pkg{keras} on \pkg{tensorflow} and treats three problems that differ
in input type, output type, and — instructively — in how well they turn out.

\subsection{Multi-output regression: CRISPR repair}
Given a 84-position DNA sequence around a Cas9 cut site, predict five properties of the
repair outcome at once: average deletion length, average insertion length, fraction of
frameshifts, fraction of insertions, and indel diversity \cite{Leenay_2019}. This is a
natural fit for the functional API, because one shared trunk can feed five separate
regression heads:

\begin{lstlisting}
## shared representation, then five named single-unit outputs
outputs <- list(
  avg_deletion_length  = layer_dense(shared, units = 1, name = "avg_deletion_length"),
  avg_insertion_length = layer_dense(shared, units = 1, name = "avg_insertion_length"),
  fraction_frameshifts = layer_dense(shared, units = 1, name = "fraction_frameshifts"),
  fraction_insertions  = layer_dense(shared, units = 1, name = "fraction_insertions"),
  indel_diversity      = layer_dense(shared, units = 1, name = "indel_diversity"))
\end{lstlisting}

Sharing a trunk encodes a real biological assumption — that the same sequence features
drive all five outcomes — and it is far more parsimonious than five separate networks: the
convolutional variant needs only 22\,195 parameters in total.

\subsection{Computer vision: flower classification}
The third problem is image classification over five flower species, and it is the one that
worked. Three architectures were compared on identical data: a dense network flattening
the raw pixels (3.69\,M parameters), a single-convolution CNN (4.77\,M), and a
two-convolution CNN with batch normalisation (9.28\,M).

\begin{lstlisting}
create_model_flower_CNN1 <- function(input_shape, num_classes) {
  keras_model_sequential() %>%
    layer_conv_2d(filters = 16, kernel_size = c(3,3), activation = 'relu',
                  input_shape = input_shape) %>%
    layer_max_pooling_2d(pool_size = c(2,2)) %>%
    layer_dropout(0.25) %>%
    layer_flatten() %>%
    layer_dense(units = 64, activation = 'relu') %>%
    layer_batch_normalization() %>%
    layer_dropout(0.5) %>%
    layer_dense(units = num_classes, activation = 'softmax') %>%
    compile(optimizer = optimizer_adam(learning_rate = 0.001),
            loss = 'sparse_categorical_crossentropy', metrics = c('accuracy'))
}
\end{lstlisting}

The convolutional design is doing something the dense network cannot: a convolution shares
one small filter across every position in the image, so it learns translation-equivariant
features with a fraction of the parameters a fully-connected layer would need for the same
receptive field. On held-out images the light CNN reached an accuracy of $0.998$ at a loss
of $0.004$.

\begin{pitfall}
The transfer-learning model is the outlier, and it is worth dwelling on. A ResNet-50 base
with frozen weights — 23.6\,M non-trainable parameters, only 525\,829 trainable in the new
head — scored $0.25$, barely above the $0.20$ expected from guessing among five classes,
while a from-scratch CNN a fifth of its size scored essentially perfectly. Transfer
learning is not free: it assumes the frozen features are appropriate to the new domain and
that the inputs are preprocessed exactly as the base model expects. When it underperforms
this badly, suspect a preprocessing mismatch or an over-aggressive freeze before concluding
anything about the data.
\end{pitfall}

Inspecting the few images where the successful model hedged is more informative than the
aggregate score. Only two test images produced a top-class probability below the
confidence threshold, and one of them was a dandelion at the seed-head stage — visually
closer to the network's idea of a rose than to the yellow flower it had learned. The model's
uncertainty was, in that instance, a faithful report about a genuinely atypical input.

\subsection{When deep learning does not work}
The CRISPR and healthcare tasks did not succeed, and the book records that deliberately.
The healthcare data are synthetically generated, so no genuine signal linking patient
attributes to test outcome exists to be found; no architecture can recover a relationship
that was never in the data. The CRISPR task is real but hard, and a network of this size
trained on this sample did not capture it.

\begin{keybox}
Two of three deep-learning problems here failed, and the failures are the more useful
result. A negative outcome separates a hard problem from an absent signal — and neither is
diagnosed by adding layers. Practical guidance for avoiding the common traps in biological
deep learning is collected by Lee et al.\ \cite{Lee_2022}; broader surveys of the field's
genomic applications are given by Liu et al.\ \cite{Liu_2020}, and the \pkg{keras} idioms
used here follow Chollet and Allaire \cite{Allaire_2018}.
\end{keybox}

\begin{pitfall}
Neural networks are the most eager over-fitters in this book. They demand standardized
inputs, explicit regularisation (weight decay, dropout, batch normalisation), and an honest
validation split. On the small tabular data typical of ecology, a random forest usually
matches or beats a neural net with a fraction of the tuning — as the AVONET comparison in
Chapter~12 found, where \code{avNNet} competed against tree ensembles and lost.
\end{pitfall}

\chapter{Large Language Models and Embeddings}
% ===========================================================================
The course ends where machine learning meets the theme of Chapter~1. A large language
model (LLM) turns each word, sentence or document into a high-dimensional vector — an
\emph{embedding} — arranged so that geometric proximity encodes \emph{meaning}. An
embedding \emph{is} a row of a multivariate data matrix, so every tool in this book
applies to it unchanged: distances, PCA/UMAP, clustering, classification.

\section{Meaning as geometry}
For unit-normalised embeddings the natural similarity is the cosine, which for unit
vectors is just the dot product:
\[
  \cos(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})=\frac{\mathbf{u}^{\!\top}\mathbf{v}}
       {\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert}.
\]
Cosine is preferred to Euclidean distance here for the same reason correlation is preferred
to covariance in Chapter~1: it discards magnitude and keeps direction. In a language model
the magnitude of a token's vector largely tracks how often the token appears, which is
rarely what we mean by similarity.

Remarkably, \emph{relations} become vector arithmetic \cite{Mikolov_2013}: the direction
from \code{man} to \code{woman} parallels \code{king} to \code{queen}, so
$\text{king}-\text{man}+\text{woman}\approx\text{queen}$.

\begin{lstlisting}
E <- <embedding matrix, one unit-row per word>   # from an LLM, or built structurally
cosine <- function(a, b) sum(a * b)              # rows are unit vectors
q <- E["king",] - E["man",] + E["woman",]
q <- q / sqrt(sum(q^2))
sort(E %*% q, decreasing = TRUE)[1:3]
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=rout]
cos(king, queen)  = 0.46
cos(king, cell)   = -0.003
cos(gene, genome) = 1

king - man + woman  ->  nearest words:
[1] 0.866 0.245 0.236
\end{lstlisting}

The three cosines confirm the geometry behaves: royalty terms are positively related,
\code{king} and \code{cell} are orthogonal to within rounding, and \code{gene}/\code{genome}
— built from the same latent axis — coincide exactly. The analogy resolves as intended,
with the top match at $0.866$ well clear of the runners-up at $0.245$ and $0.236$.

\begin{pitfall}
Those three numbers arrive unlabelled, and that is a property of the code rather than of
the mathematics: \code{sort()} applied to the one-column matrix \code{E \%*\% q} discards
the row names before the result is printed. Recovering the words requires sorting an
explicitly named vector, e.g.\ \code{sort(setNames(drop(E \%*\% q), rownames(E)))}. It is a
small thing, but it is the kind of small thing that turns a reproducible result into an
uncheckable one.
\end{pitfall}

\section{Where the vectors come from}
Nothing above says how an embedding is produced. Modern language models build them with
\emph{attention} \cite{Vaswani_2017}. Each token is projected into three matrices — queries
$\mathbf{Q}$, keys $\mathbf{K}$ and values $\mathbf{V}$ — and the representation of a token
is a weighted average of all the values in the sequence:
\[
  \operatorname{Attention}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V})
    =\operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\!\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)\mathbf{V}.
\]
The inner product $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\!\top}$ is a similarity matrix between every pair
of positions — a Q-mode matrix in the vocabulary of Chapter~1 — and the softmax turns each
of its rows into weights summing to one. The scaling by $\sqrt{d_k}$ keeps those inner
products from growing with dimension and driving the softmax into saturation. The result is
a representation whose meaning depends on context, so that the vector for a word is no
longer fixed but shaped by the sentence it sits in.

The same architecture is what carried deep learning into structural biology: AlphaFold
\cite{Jumper_2021} applies attention over residues and evolutionary couplings to predict
protein structure at atomic accuracy — a reminder that the embedding idea is not confined
to text.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.62\linewidth]{27_llm_embeddings.png}
  \caption{A PCA projection of word embeddings. Semantically related words cluster —
  royalty/gender, geography, animals, biology — because the embedding places meaning in
  geometry. The same PCA, distances and clustering from earlier chapters now operate on
  language.}
  \label{fig:llm}
\end{figure}

\begin{keybox}
Nothing in this chapter is new machinery — it is the \emph{data matrix} of Chapter~1,
populated by a language model instead of a caliper. Transformers \cite{Vaswani_2017}
produce the vectors; the multivariate toolbox of this book interprets them. That is the
throughline of the course: master the geometry of the data matrix, and every method,
classical or modern, becomes a variation on one theme.
\end{keybox}

\chapter{Ecological Niche Modeling}
% ===========================================================================
An ecological niche model (ENM) estimates the environmental conditions where a species
can occur. Statistically it is a \emph{binomial regression} — the GLM of Chapter~11 —
whose response is presence/absence (or presence/background) and whose predictors are
climate variables. The fitted surface, projected onto geography, becomes a suitability
map. The full classroom pipeline uses spatial rasters (\pkg{terra}, \pkg{dismo},
WorldClim); here we distil the statistical core so it runs without GIS layers.

\begin{lstlisting}
occ  <- get_data("hanta")               # Sp (0/1), bio_1, bio_12
enm2 <- glm(Sp ~ bio_1 + bio_12, data = occ, family = binomial)
enmq <- glm(Sp ~ poly(bio_1, 2) + bio_12, data = occ, family = binomial)
AIC(enm2, enmq)                         # does a hump-shaped temperature response help?
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=rout]
Best-model coefficients (bio_1 + bio_12):
(Intercept)       bio_1      bio_12
   -2.15504     0.13201    -0.00152

Training AUC (bio_1 + bio_12): 0.843     # good discrimination (0.5 = random, 1 = perfect)
\end{lstlisting}

The positive \code{bio\_1} coefficient means warmer sites are more suitable; the small
negative \code{bio\_12} term slightly penalises the wettest sites. An AUC of $0.843$ says
the model ranks a random presence above a random absence about $84\%$ of the time.
Figure~\ref{fig:enm} shows the two standard ENM outputs: a \emph{response curve} (how
suitability varies along one predictor) and a \emph{suitability surface} in environmental
space, with observed detections overlaid.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{22_enm.png}
  \caption{Ecological niche model. Left: response curve of presence probability against
  temperature. Right: the fitted suitability surface over the two bioclimatic axes;
  detections (points) fall in the high-suitability region.}
  \label{fig:enm}
\end{figure}

\begin{pitfall}
Presence-only data has no true absences; the ``0''s are usually \emph{background} points.
Treating background as genuine absence biases prevalence and inflates apparent
performance. And AUC computed on the training data (as here, for illustration) is
optimistic — always report AUC on spatially independent held-out data.
\end{pitfall}

\chapter{Presence--Absence Matrices}
% ===========================================================================
A presence--absence matrix (PAM) records which species occur at which sites as a binary
matrix — the discrete cousin of the community tables from Correspondence Analysis. Its
two marginal sums are the fundamental quantities of biodiversity: row sums are
\emph{richness} (species per site, $\alpha$) and column sums are \emph{range size}
(sites per species, $\omega$).

\begin{lstlisting}
pamdf <- get_data("pam")
pam   <- as.matrix(pamdf[, -1])         # sites x species (0/1)
alpha <- rowSums(pam)                    # richness per site
omega <- colSums(pam)                    # range size per species
c(fill = mean(pam), mean_alpha = mean(alpha), mean_omega = mean(omega))
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=rout]
       fill  mean_alpha  mean_omega
      0.327       39.27       26.18       # 80 sites x 120 species, 33% filled
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{24_pam.png}
  \caption{A presence--absence matrix (left, sites ordered by latitude), the richness
  gradient it implies ($\alpha$ per site, middle), and the frequency distribution of
  range sizes ($\omega$ per species, right).}
  \label{fig:pam}
\end{figure}

\begin{keybox}
The matrix product $\mathbf{P}\mathbf{P}^{\!\top}$ counts \emph{shared species} between
every pair of sites, and $\mathbf{P}^{\!\top}\mathbf{P}$ counts \emph{co-occurrences}
between every pair of species. From these two products fall out $\beta$-diversity, dispersion
fields, and range--diversity plots — the whole macroecology of the PAM is linear algebra
on a binary matrix, closing the loop back to Chapter~1.
\end{keybox}

\chapter{Reproducing this book}
Everything here is generated from the \code{MS\_LJMR/} package.

\textbf{Layout.}
\code{R/} holds the revised, portable course scripts (no \code{setwd()}, no \code{x11()};
data obtained through \code{get\_data()}). \code{verify/} holds the Python reproductions
that cross-check every method and render the figures. \code{figs/} holds those figures.
\code{book/} holds this single \code{.tex} file and \code{references.bib}. \code{data/} is
where the real course data sets go — when present they are used automatically.
\code{envs/} holds the conda environment specifications.

\textbf{Environment.} The classical chapters need only \R{} and a handful of CRAN packages.
The deep-learning chapter additionally needs \pkg{keras} bound to a working Python
TensorFlow, which is the one genuinely awkward dependency in the book. A single conda
environment covers everything:

\begin{lstlisting}
mamba env create -f envs/environment.yml   # or conda env create
conda activate msljmr
R -e 'library(tensorflow); tf$constant("ok")'   # verifies the R -> Python -> TF chain
\end{lstlisting}

\code{envs/environment-gpu.yml} is the CUDA variant, and \code{envs/README.md} explains the
version pinning — the R binding and the Python engine must move together.

\textbf{To rebuild the figures:}
\begin{lstlisting}
cd MS_LJMR                   # run from the repo root so R/00_utils.R resolves
for f in R/*.R; do Rscript "$f"; done   # figures land in figs/

## or, for the independent Python cross-check:
cd verify
python3 make_figures.py      # core chapters + verification log
python3 extra_figures.py     # dissimilarity, correlation, MDS figures
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{To rebuild this book:} the bibliography uses classic \textsc{Bib}\TeX{}, so the
sequence is

\begin{lstlisting}
cd MS_LJMR/book
pdflatex MS_LJMR_Multivariate.tex
bibtex   MS_LJMR_Multivariate
pdflatex MS_LJMR_Multivariate.tex
pdflatex MS_LJMR_Multivariate.tex   # resolves citations, cross-references and the TOC
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{Verification.} The classical chapters were cross-checked by recomputing each
method's key quantities independently in Python (NumPy / SciPy / scikit-learn / statsmodels)
and logging them to \code{verify/verify\_log.txt}; the \R{} scripts were written to match.
Representative reference values: iris PCA variance $73.0/22.9/3.7/0.5\%$; $k$-means recovers
species with $89\%$ agreement; LDA hold-out accuracy $1.00$ on the four-group morphometrics.
The machine-learning chapters report measurements from the course units they describe rather
than from the small companion scripts: the anuran held-out accuracy of $0.90$, the AVONET
figures of $0.79$ accuracy and $\kappa=0.64$, and the flower-classification accuracy of
$0.998$ at a loss of $0.004$ are all recorded outputs of those analyses.
